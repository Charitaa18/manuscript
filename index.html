Designerly ways of knowing

Nigel Cross

1982

A principal outcome of the Royal College of Art’s research project on ‘Design in general education’ was the restatement of a belief in a missing ‘third area’ of education1. The two already-established areas can be broadly classified as education in the sciences and education in the arts, or humanities. These ‘two cultures’ have long been recognised as dominating our social, cultural and educational systems.

According to the author there’s a duopoly when it comes to education. The two main recognised cultures are the sciences and the arts/humanities. He feels and I agree, that there is a 3rd culture missing from this system, which is Design. Mainly consisting of planning, inventing, making, and doing. He seems to be advocating for positioning design up there with science and humanities as a fundamental cognitive tool.

The central concern of Design is ‘the conception and realisation of new things’.
  • It encompasses the appreciation of ‘material culture’ and the application of ‘the arts of planning, inventing, making and doing’.
  • At its core is the ‘language’ of ‘modelling’; it is possible to develop students’ aptitudes in this ‘language’, equivalent to aptitudes in the ‘language’ of the sciences - numeracy - and the ‘language’ of humanities - literacy.
  • Design has its own distinct ‘things to know, ways of knowing them, and ways of finding out about them’. Even a ‘three cultures’ view of human knowledge and ability is a simple model.

    • It literally forms and shapes the world around us on a large scale and is the basis behind the everyday objects and systems we use on a daily basis.

    Start with something small like a lamp post in a park, it’s design, location within the park, functional properties of the bulb, aesthetics or visual feel, etc are all taken into account. Zoom out a bit, and the park with it’s walkways, landscaping, biking & car routes, dog parks all designed keeping daily utility of human experience in mind.

    Zoom out further and you have a city, the grid, roads and highways leading in and out, layout of commercial, residential and industrial areas, and spread of important service centres such as hospitals,police stations and schools all require planning and design. So the fact that you have to predict or comprehend or interpret the human experience while making your decisions is what makes design unique when compared to the other systems of education.

    It is largely a matter of “knowing how” rather than of “knowing that”10 - of knack rather than understanding. Furthermore what there is to know throws very little light on much else.’ This is therefore a challenging criterion for design education, since design is often regarded as a skill, perhaps something like bicycle-riding, swimming, or playing golf. I would now accept Peter’s suggestion that: An ‘educated man’ is distinguished not as much by what he does as by what he ‘sees’ or ‘grasps’. If he does something very well, in which he has to be trained, he must see this in perspective, as related to other things.

    We discussed this in our Designing for Usability class as well, the one quality every designer must possess is EMPATHY. I feel like you can branch outwards from this trait to other characteristics like functionality/ practicality and innovation/ ingenuity. (Thereis a difference between “knowing how” and “knowing that” as mentioned in the text).

    A central feature of design activity, then, is its reliance on generating fairly quickly a satisfactory solution, rather than on any prolonged analysis of the problem. In Simon’s13 inelegant term, it is a process of ‘satisficing’ rather than optimising; producing any one of what might well be a large range of satisfactory solutions rather than attempting to generate the one hypothetically-optimum solution.

    Another point the author makes that I highly relate to is that there is no fixed “right answer” in design. It’s highly dependent on the context. Yes, there are standards set in place but there is room to reinvent the object or system itself such that the standards have to be rewired. As a designer you must determine what the best solution is for the problem you are trying to tackle.

    Perhaps it would be better to regard the ‘third culture’ as technology, rather than design. This ‘material culture’ of design is, after all, the culture of the technologist - of the designer, doer and maker. Technology involves a synthesis of knowledge and skills from both the sciences and the humanities, in the pursuit of practical tasks; it is not simply ‘applied science’, but ‘the application of scientific and other organised knowledge to practical tasks…

    One interesting part in the book the author talks about how design could be seen as a synthesis of both the sciences and humanities. For example: When you build a bridge, you have to understand the physics behind how the structure would stand, hold weight. And the understanding behind how people would use it in their everyday life. 

    Traditionally, design teachers have been practising designers who pass on their knowledge, skills and values through a process of apprenticeship. Design students ‘act out’ the role of designer in small projects6, and are tutored in the process by more experienced designers. These design teachers are firstly designers, and only secondly and incidentally teachers. This model may be defensible for specialist education7, but in general education all teachers are (or should be) firstly teachers, and only secondly, if at all, specialists in any field

    The author also mentions that he believes there’s a flaw with the apprenticeship model, how the teachers are practicing designers first and teachers second. I disagree because I feel like the real world experience would help any educator teach better. A lot of the times, we’re taught to design and think without constraints. I personally don’t believe this to be an effective method. Creating real world constraints and environments would actually help students understand how to work more practically.

    Lawson’s studies of design behaviour, in particular, have compared the problem-solving strategies of designers with those of scientists.11 He devised problems which required the arrangement of 3D coloured blocks so as to satisfy certain rules (some of which were not initially disclosed), and set the same problems to both postgraduate architectural students and postgraduate science students. The two groups showed dissimilar problem-solving strategies, according to Lawson.

    The block experiment is another passage that stood out to me. Here the author points out that scientists Vs designers, when given the same block experiment had 2 completely different strategies. Scientists used a problem focused strategy and designers took a more solution focused approach (or a trial and error method). While the claim is that the designer figured out more about the larger nature of the problem while trying out different solutions (something I relate to) when it came to combining this with the concept of speed over analysis I had a different view.

    Here I would say I wouldn’t align completely with either party, while creating visual solutions and building off them feels more productive, I’ve found that skipping over/ rushing through the analytical portion of the problem leads to more issues in the future. My undergrad professor drilled this into our heads “think twice, cut/draw once”.

    I understand that designers unlike scientists have quicker deadlines and are expected to show tangible results sooner. But in my opinion, spending that extra bit of time trying to empathise, analyse beforehand can help save a lot of time in the future. 

    Jones has commented that ‘changing the problem in order to find a solution is the most challenging and difficult part of designing.

    This line really stood out to me, because it talks about changing your perspective so far round that you end up redefining the problem itself but still finding a solution that tackles your overall problem. I looked up this concept to better understand what the author was trying to say and found a few simple but fascinating examples.

    Cubtankthink.com

    Uber when it first launched was trying to tackle an issue most cab companies faced with people cancelling their rides constantly. Anything over 5 minutes apparently. Their first strategy was to find ways to optimise travel, find quicker routes and study traffic patterns. They were coming up short. Then they decided to tackle this problem differently. Why is it that people were cancelling? The understood the basic drive behind the human psychology of the unknown. The wait time felt longer because the customers were staring at blank screens.So a new problem was posed, how do they make the wait time feel more engaging and stress free?

    They added a screen that showed real time location of the drivers relative to the customer. This paired with a countdown timer that said “Driver arriving in 6 minutes”, “driver arriving in 4 minutes” etc gave users visual feedback in real time. The cancellations dropped significantly.

    Firstround.com

    According to this interview with co-founder of Airbnb, Joe Gebbia published in the executive journal “First round Review” he talks about how they tried to solve their problem of stagnant growth and a lack of traffic on their platform “the usual way” and on a scalable level (using code/marketing strategies) for a year before the structural shift in their perspective. They decided to redefine the problem through the lens of human experience. They sent a 3 man team to actual customers and figured out the problem was actually something else entirely. Poor quality photos and their willingness to get “their hands dirty” and solve the problem by taking and uploading professional pictures of the houses themselves saved their business from potentially flatlining. 

    This was a fascinating concept to me because I guess as designers we also tend to get stuck on one train of thought. I sometimes get stuck on a solution because I’ve already envisioned a specific output/ end result. And it’s hard to switch or change my process because I’m working backwards. So this idea of changing the problem itself is unique and fresh perspective that I think I could truly benefit from implementing in my design practice.

    The book mentions that as designers we have to find comfort in the chaos and uncertainty. Honing the “designerly ways of thinking” is exactly what’s needed to cultivate a fluid and adaptive mindset. To truly master the art of being a designer.
